When You Pray for So Long That You Begin to Hurt

An honest conversation with Justin about trauma, anger, justice, and prayer.

Some prayers come from a place of hope, while others come from wounds that have never fully healed. When someone has experienced something deeply painful, the desire for justice can become tangled with anger, disappointment, and questions about why things happened the way they did.

When I asked Justin whether he had ever prayed for something for so long that he began to wonder whether he should keep asking, his answer took him back to a traumatic experience that had affected him deeply.

Brad: Have you ever prayed for something for a long time and reached a point where you wondered whether you should keep asking God for it?

Justin: Yes. It reminds me of what happened with my ex. I was beaten so badly that there was blood everywhere, and it went on for nearly three hours.

Brad: When you think back to that situation, what stays with you the most?

Justin: I remember his mother walking into the picture while everything was happening. I felt that she should have thought about me as another mother's child, too. My sister was about two hours away, and I called her while it was happening. She was trying to get to me.

Brad: What happened after that, when the situation moved into the legal system?

Justin: There was a lot to deal with involving law enforcement and the court proceedings. I remember being in court for the ruling involving the domestic violence felony charge in Kentucky. I watched the outcome, including the probation and the fees he had to deal with for the courts and attorneys. I struggled with how everything unfolded and felt that the consequences did not reflect what I believed had happened.

Brad: How did all of that affect what you were praying for?

Justin: I prayed that life would hurt the person who had hurt me. I wanted there to be consequences. I wanted the person to understand what I had gone through.

Brad: When you were carrying all of that anger, how did it affect you personally?

Justin: I became so angry that I threatened to burn my house down. There was a lot going on, and I eventually went through therapy.

Brad: Looking back, what do you think you needed most during that time?

Justin: I needed to work through what had happened and deal with the anger I was carrying. I couldn't just keep holding everything inside.

What This Conversation Left Me Thinking About

As I listened to Justin, I thought about how pain can change the things we ask God for. When someone has been deeply hurt, the desire for justice can become difficult to separate from the desire for the other person to experience that same pain.

Justin was honest about wanting life to hurt the person who had hurt him. That honesty matters because anger is not always something people feel comfortable admitting, especially when they are trying to reconcile their feelings with their faith.

His experience also reminded me that we cannot assume we understand another person's pain simply because we know what happened. The assault itself was traumatic, but the memories surrounding it, the involvement of family, and the subsequent court proceedings were also part of the experience he had to process.

Romans 12:19 speaks about leaving vengeance to God rather than taking it into our own hands. That does not mean ignoring wrongdoing or abandoning accountability. It means recognizing that seeking justice and living for another person's suffering are not the same thing.

I also found it significant that Justin went through therapy. Prayer and practical support do not have to compete with each other. A person can seek God while also receiving help to work through trauma, anger, and the lasting effects of a painful experience.

James 4:2 asks us to consider the importance of asking, but Justin's story raises another question. What happens when we continue asking God for an outcome that we cannot control?

We may want someone to acknowledge the harm they caused. We may want a legal outcome that feels fair. We may want the other person to understand the pain we have experienced. Yet we cannot determine every consequence or make another person respond as we believe they should.

That does not mean we must pretend the harm never happened. It means we must find a way to address what happened without allowing the desire for revenge to consume us.

Justin's experience brought me back to the difference between asking God for justice and asking for someone else to suffer. Both desires can emerge from the same wound, but they can lead us in very different directions.

Sometimes we continue asking because we want circumstances to change. At other times, we need to recognize how deeply those circumstances have affected us. We may still have questions, and we may not understand every outcome, but we can seek help as we work through what we have carried.

Perhaps continuing to ask God does not always mean asking Him to change another person's life. Sometimes it also means asking for help as we face the pain that remains in our own.
