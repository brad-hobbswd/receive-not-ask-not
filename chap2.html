Chapter Two

When We Have Asked Before

There is something different about asking for something when you have never been disappointed and asking for it after you have prayed about it a hundred times.

The first time, you may feel hopeful. You believe something can change, and you approach God with the expectation that He hears you. But when the days turn into months, and the months turn into years, something can begin to change inside you. You may continue praying, but the words do not come as easily. You may still believe God can answer, but you are no longer sure what to expect.

Eventually, you may find yourself wondering whether it is worth asking again.

I think this is a part of prayer that deserves an honest conversation. We hear about persistence, faith, and trusting God, but we do not always talk about the emotional weight of waiting for something that matters deeply to us.

What happens when you have asked God to restore a relationship, change a circumstance, provide an opportunity, or bring healing into your life, and the outcome you hoped for never arrives?

Do you keep asking? Do you change the way you pray? Do you begin to question yourself, your faith, or your understanding of God?

Or do you quietly stop asking altogether?

These questions are not always easy to answer because disappointment does not affect everyone in the same way. Some people draw closer to God during difficult seasons. Others struggle to pray. Some continue believing while carrying questions they cannot resolve. And some need time to acknowledge that they are hurting before they can even begin to talk about faith again.

Jesus spoke about the importance of persistent prayer in Luke 18:1, explaining that people ought always to pray and not lose heart. Those words are meaningful, especially when we consider how easily discouragement can settle into a person's life. Yet persistence does not mean pretending that waiting never hurts. It does not require us to deny our questions or act as though disappointment has no effect on us.

Sometimes faith looks like continuing to pray. Sometimes it begins with admitting that we are struggling to pray at all.

I want to understand what happens in the space between asking and receiving, especially when that space becomes longer than we ever expected.

I want to hear from people who have waited, people who have wrestled with disappointment, and people who have had to reconsider what they believed prayer would accomplish. I want to understand what kept them going, what made them want to give up, and whether their understanding of God changed along the way.

Because there is a difference between telling someone to keep praying and understanding what it costs them to do so.

Perhaps the question is not simply whether we have asked before. Perhaps it is what repeated disappointment has taught us to expect the next time we ask.

And that is where the conversation begins.

When the Pain Becomes Part of the Prayer

To understand what repeated disappointment can do to a person's prayer life, I spoke with Justin. His experience brought this chapter's question into a very different kind of focus. He was not describing an ordinary delay or a request that had simply gone unanswered. He was trying to make sense of a traumatic experience, the legal process that followed, and the anger that remained when the outcome did not feel like justice.

My conversation with Justin helped me understand that repeated prayer is not always about waiting for a circumstance to change. Sometimes it is about carrying the memory of what happened while trying to understand what justice should look like. When someone has been deeply hurt, the desire for an answer can become mixed with anger, grief, and the longing for the person responsible to understand the damage they caused.

Justin described an assault that left him with lasting pain. He also spoke about the people who became part of that experience, the fear and urgency of trying to reach his sister, and the legal process that followed. The court outcome did not feel to him like it reflected the seriousness of what he had endured. That sense of injustice became part of what he carried into prayer.

He was honest about what he asked for. He wanted life to hurt the person who had hurt him. He wanted consequences, and he wanted that person to understand his suffering. That is not an easy thing to say out loud, but it is an important part of his story. Pain can make revenge feel like the only outcome that would be fair. When we have been wounded deeply, we may find ourselves asking God not only to make things right, but to make the other person feel what we felt.

But there is a difference between asking for justice and asking for revenge. Justice recognizes that harm matters, that wrongdoing should not be excused, and that accountability has a place. Revenge makes another person's suffering the measure of whether our own pain has been answered. One seeks what is right; the other can keep us tied to the very harm we are trying to escape.

Romans 12:19 reminds us not to take vengeance into our own hands, but to leave room for God's judgment. This does not ask us to deny what happened, remain in danger, abandon lawful accountability, or pretend that the consequences were fair. It calls us to recognize that vengeance is not ours to carry out. We can pursue safety, seek appropriate justice, tell the truth, and still ask God to keep bitterness and retaliation from taking over our hearts.

Justin also shared that his anger reached a frightening point and that he eventually went through therapy. That part of the conversation matters. Healing from trauma is not always immediate, and it is not a sign of weak faith to need help. Prayer can be part of the process, and so can therapy, trusted support, and practical steps toward safety and recovery. We do not have to choose between seeking God and receiving help.

So what happens when we have asked before and the pain is still there? We may need to keep bringing the truth of that pain to God, while allowing Him to work on more than the circumstances outside us. We can ask for justice without asking to be consumed by revenge. We can ask for relief without pretending the wound is small. We can ask for strength to face what happened and wisdom to take the next right step, even when we cannot control the outcome.

Justin's experience does not offer a simple explanation for every unanswered prayer, and it should not be treated as a formula for how every person heals. It does show us that the question can change over time. At first, we may ask God to make the situation right. Later, we may also need to ask Him to help us live without letting what happened define every part of our future.

Perhaps persistence in prayer is not measured only by how many times we repeat the same request. Sometimes it is found in our willingness to keep coming honestly before God, including with the anger, grief, and questions we would rather hide. We may not receive the outcome we imagined, but we can seek help for the pain we are still carrying.

Justin's experience does not answer every question about suffering, justice, or unanswered prayer. It does make the questions in this chapter personal. His conversation shows what it can look like when the pain remains long after the event itself, and when a person is still trying to understand what to ask God for next.
