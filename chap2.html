Chapter Two

When We Have Asked Before

There is something different about asking for something when you have never been disappointed and asking for it after you have prayed about it a hundred times.

The first time, you may feel hopeful. You believe something can change, and you approach God with the expectation that He hears you. But when the days turn into months, and the months turn into years, something can begin to change inside you. You may continue praying, but the words do not come as easily. You may still believe God can answer, but you are no longer sure what to expect.

Eventually, you may find yourself wondering whether it is worth asking again.

I think this is a part of prayer that deserves an honest conversation. We hear about persistence, faith, and trusting God, but we do not always talk about the emotional weight of waiting for something that matters deeply to us.

What happens when you have asked God to restore a relationship, change a circumstance, provide an opportunity, or bring healing into your life, and the outcome you hoped for never arrives?

Do you keep asking? Do you change the way you pray? Do you begin to question yourself, your faith, or your understanding of God?

Or do you quietly stop asking altogether?

These questions are not always easy to answer because disappointment does not affect everyone in the same way. Some people draw closer to God during difficult seasons. Others struggle to pray. Some continue believing while carrying questions they cannot resolve. And some need time to acknowledge that they are hurting before they can even begin to talk about faith again.

Jesus spoke about the importance of persistent prayer in Luke 18:1, explaining that people ought always to pray and not lose heart. Those words are meaningful, especially when we consider how easily discouragement can settle into a person's life. Yet persistence does not mean pretending that waiting never hurts. It does not require us to deny our questions or act as though disappointment has no effect on us.

Sometimes faith looks like continuing to pray. Sometimes it begins with admitting that we are struggling to pray at all.

I want to understand what happens in the space between asking and receiving, especially when that space becomes longer than we ever expected.

I want to hear from people who have waited, people who have wrestled with disappointment, and people who have had to reconsider what they believed prayer would accomplish. I want to understand what kept them going, what made them want to give up, and whether their understanding of God changed along the way.

Because there is a difference between telling someone to keep praying and understanding what it costs them to do so.

Perhaps the question is not simply whether we have asked before. Perhaps it is what repeated disappointment has taught us to expect the next time we ask.

And that is where the conversation begins.
