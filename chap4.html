Chapter Four

What Are We Really Asking For?

Sometimes we know exactly what we want God to change. We want a relationship restored, a difficult situation resolved, a door opened, or a person to finally understand how much they have hurt us. We pray about the circumstances because those are the things we can see, the things that affect us every day.

But have we ever stopped to consider what lies beneath the request?

Sometimes we ask for peace because we are exhausted by conflict. Sometimes we ask for a relationship to change because we are afraid of losing it. Sometimes we ask for another person to see things differently because we cannot understand why they continue to hurt us. Our requests may be genuine, yet the emotions behind them can be more complicated than we first realize.

I have wondered how often we focus so much on the outcome we want that we overlook what the experience is revealing about us. We want God to change the situation, but we may not be ready to examine our expectations, our reactions, or the fears influencing our decisions.

That does not mean every difficult circumstance is our fault. Someone else's harmful choices are not automatically a reflection of something we have done wrong. We must be careful not to turn personal reflection into self blame. Yet we can acknowledge that, even when we are not responsible for what happened to us, we still have choices about how we respond and what we allow to shape our lives.

James 4:3 adds another dimension to the words that inspired this book. It speaks about asking with wrong motives. That challenges us to look beyond the request itself and consider what we hope to accomplish through it.

Do we want reconciliation because we genuinely desire healing, or are we hoping that another person will finally become who we need them to be? Are we asking for justice, or have we begun to desire revenge? Do we want a new opportunity because it aligns with our purpose, or because we believe it will finally prove our worth to someone else?

These questions are not always comfortable, and they do not have the same answer for everyone. Sometimes we discover that our desires are healthy and sincere. Other times, we realize that disappointment, fear, or resentment has become part of what we are asking for.

I think these are conversations worth having because prayer is not simply about presenting a list of things we want to change. It is also an opportunity to become honest about our desires and the reasons behind them.

Perhaps the most difficult question is not always whether God will give us what we are asking for. Perhaps it is whether we are willing to examine why we want it in the first place.

And sometimes that examination reveals something we had not expected to find.

What Ashley's Story Helps Us See

My conversation with Ashley helped me understand how complicated the desire for change can become when someone has lived through one difficult season after another. She spoke openly about relationships that did not become what she hoped they would be, about leaving a situation that was affecting her health and well-being, and about the difficult decisions that followed. Her story is not a simple list of choices that can be judged from a distance. It is the account of a person trying to find a way forward while carrying responsibilities and circumstances that were not easy to navigate.

When Ashley described what life looks like now, she also spoke about the steps she has taken toward greater stability. She has employment, transportation, and a goal of saving toward a home. Those details matter because they remind us that change is not always one dramatic moment. Sometimes it is a series of practical steps, decisions, and efforts to build a different future.

Her experience brings us back to the question at the heart of this chapter: What are we really asking God to change? We may begin by asking for a relationship to improve, for another person to act differently, or for life to become easier. Those requests may come from genuine needs for love, security, peace, and stability. But we may also need to ask for wisdom to recognize what is within our control, courage to make difficult decisions, and clarity about what is healthy and safe.

That kind of reflection must never become a way of blaming someone for being mistreated. Ashley's experience does not prove that she caused the difficulties she faced, nor does it give anyone permission to reduce her life to a judgment about her relationships. Another person's harmful behavior remains their responsibility. Examining our own motives is not the same as accepting blame for someone else's actions.

James 4:3 challenges us to look honestly at the desires behind our requests. It asks us to consider whether what we want is shaped by selfishness, resentment, fear, or a desire to satisfy something that cannot give us lasting peace. But the passage should lead us toward honest self-examination, not condemnation. We can ask God to reveal what is happening in our hearts while also asking Him for protection, direction, provision, and the strength to take wise steps.

Ashley's story also reminds us that prayer and action belong together. Asking God for change does not mean waiting passively while life unfolds around us. There are moments when the next faithful step involves seeking support, caring for our health, setting boundaries, making a difficult decision, or working patiently toward a more stable future. We may not control every circumstance, but we can seek wisdom for the choices before us.

We cannot decide what another person will become, and we cannot guarantee how every situation will end. We can, however, become more honest about what we are seeking and why. We can ask God not only to change what surrounds us, but to guide our response, correct our motives where correction is needed, and help us recognize the difference between what we desire and what is truly good for us.

Ashley's conversation does not provide a formula for every person's situation. It gives us a real account that invites us to look beyond appearances and consider the complexity of another person's life. Read “When All You Want Is for Life to Change” to hear the conversation with Ashley and the experiences behind these reflections.
