The One Everyone Depends On

Bradley Hobbs reflects on growing up with responsibility, caring for family, navigating relationships, and learning to care for himself.

Sometimes the questions we ask other people eventually find their way back to us. When Justin asked me whether I had ever felt like I needed to be strong for everyone else, even while struggling myself, I did not have to search very far for an answer.

Justin: Have you ever felt like you had to be strong for everyone else, even when you were struggling yourself?

Bradley: Yes, and when I think about it, I realize that this started when I was young. I don't know that I ever really had much time to think about whether I was struggling. There were things that needed to be done, and I had to keep going.

My mother had cancer and was sick, while my dad was away doing his own thing. I had sisters to help raise, school to attend, and homework to complete whenever I could find the time. When you're young, you don't necessarily understand everything that's happening around you. You just know that people need you, and you try to do what you can.

Justin: Did you feel like you had a choice?

Bradley: Honestly, I was focused on what needed to be done. I had responsibilities at home, but I also knew education mattered. As I got older, college became important to me, too. I was trying to build a future while still carrying responsibilities from my family life.

After my mother passed away, those responsibilities didn't simply disappear. My sister would come to me when she needed help, and I tried to be there for her. There were times when I also had to deal with my dad when he wanted to be involved. I would clean, help with things around the house, and fix technology problems when they came up. It was just part of what I did.

Justin: It sounds like being the person people could depend on became a normal part of your life.

Bradley: It did. Then life moved into another season. My wife had a baby, and I wanted to make sure she was taken care of afterward. I was working, too, so I was trying to balance those responsibilities with everything happening at home.

Eventually, there was a divorce, and I went through stressful working environments. Life kept bringing new challenges, and I kept trying to work through them.

Justin: Did that continue into your relationships afterward?

Bradley: Yes. When my partner was dealing with trauma from a previous relationship, I wanted to be supportive. I understood that what someone has experienced can affect them long after the situation itself has ended. I wanted to help, but I also had my own experiences and struggles to work through.

I went to therapy myself, and I was dealing with several health problems at the same time. There was a lot happening, and I had to recognize that I needed support, too.

Justin: Looking back at everything you've experienced, what do you think you have learned about being strong for other people?

Bradley: I think I learned that being there for people is important, but you cannot assume that because you are helping everyone else, you are automatically taking care of yourself. Sometimes you get so used to being the person who handles things that you don't stop to consider what everything is costing you.

I have had to work through my own experiences, and I have learned that getting help is not something to be ashamed of. Therapy was part of that process for me, and I had to acknowledge my own needs instead of focusing only on what everyone else needed.

And honestly, things are good now. I have been through difficult seasons, but those experiences are not the whole story of my life.

What I Have Come to Understand

When I look back at my life, I can see how early responsibility became part of who I was. I was trying to be present for my family while navigating school, growing into adulthood, pursuing college, and eventually building a life of my own. Each season brought different responsibilities, but the expectation that I would keep going seemed to follow me.

I do not share these experiences because I believe that carrying more than others makes someone stronger or more valuable. I share them because I know what it can feel like to keep showing up when there is a great deal happening beneath the surface.

There were times when I was caring for others while dealing with my own challenges. I wanted to support my family, be present in my relationships, and fulfill my responsibilities at work. Yet I also had to recognize that I was a person with needs of my own.

That realization matters when we return to the subject of asking.

James 4:2 reminds us of the importance of asking God, but asking requires us to acknowledge that there is something we need. For someone who has spent years being the dependable person, that admission may not come naturally. We may know how to respond when someone else needs us, yet struggle to recognize when we need help ourselves.

I have also learned that asking for help does not mean abandoning responsibility. It means being honest about what we can carry and recognizing when we need support. My experience with therapy helped me take my own struggles seriously instead of assuming that I should simply keep pushing through them.

Looking back, I can appreciate the ways I tried to be there for others without pretending that every decision or season was easy. I can also acknowledge that my life is not defined only by what I have endured. There is room for growth, healing, gratitude, and the realization that difficult seasons do not have to remain the center of our lives.

Perhaps being strong is not simply about how much we can endure for other people. Perhaps it also involves learning when to speak honestly, when to accept help, and when to recognize that our own well-being matters, too.

Sometimes the person everyone depends on needs to learn that it is okay to depend on someone else.
