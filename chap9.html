# Chapter 8A ## When Life Does Not Wait for You to Grieve ### A Conversation About My Dad When my mom died, my dad had to find a way to keep going. Life did not stop because he had lost his wife, and the responsibilities she had managed did not simply disappear. He had to pick up where she had left off and learn things he had never really learned before. He tried to keep it together, but eventually, as the saying goes, things went west. Or, as some people might put it, the waste hit the fan. Sometimes life has a way of forcing us to face everything at once. Grief is already difficult enough, but when everyday responsibilities are added to it, a person can find themselves trying to survive circumstances they never expected to face. My dad had to learn as he went. He was trying to manage life without my mom while figuring out things that had previously been taken care of by her. He did not have all the answers, and he could not suddenly become an expert at everything she had done. He had to work things out one step at a time. Eventually, help came from his own mother. That detail matters because sometimes we imagine that asking for help is something people should know how to do from the beginning. In reality, some people keep trying on their own until they reach a point where they need someone to step in. They may not know what to ask for, or they may believe they should be able to manage because everyone is already looking to them to keep going. ### A Reflection Watching someone navigate life after losing a spouse can reveal how much one person may have carried within a family. When my mom died, my dad did not simply lose his wife. He also had to adjust to responsibilities and routines that had been part of their life together. He had to learn things he had never needed to learn before, and he had to do it while facing a loss that changed his world. I cannot know every thought my dad carried during that time, but I know he tried to keep things together. Perhaps he believed he could manage, or perhaps he was simply doing what he thought had to be done. Whatever was going through his mind, there came a point when things became too much to handle alone, and help eventually came from his mother. There is something important in that part of the story. Help did not arrive because he had successfully mastered every responsibility. It came because he needed help. Sometimes we do not recognize how much a person is struggling because they continue showing up and trying to manage what is in front of them. We see the effort, but we may not see the exhaustion underneath it. We see someone carrying on, and we assume they are coping better than they really are. Grief can make ordinary responsibilities feel unfamiliar. Tasks that once seemed simple can become overwhelming when the person who shared them with you is no longer there. Even learning something practical can carry an emotional weight when it reminds you of what has changed. This is why I think we need to be careful about expecting people to recover quickly or immediately know how to rebuild their lives after a loss. Sometimes they need time. Sometimes they need someone to show them how to do something they have never done before. Sometimes they need practical help more than another reminder to be strong. My dad's story also reminds me that receiving help does not erase the effort someone has already made. He tried to keep things together. He attempted to take on what had been left behind. But eventually, his mother helped him, and he did not have to figure out everything by himself. There is no shame in that. Galatians 6:2 tells us to bear one another's burdens. Sometimes we think of that verse only in terms of prayer or emotional encouragement, but helping someone carry a burden can also mean showing them how to handle a responsibility, stepping in when they are overwhelmed, or staying close while they learn a new way of living. We cannot always remove another person's grief. We cannot replace the person they lost or make every unfamiliar responsibility easy. But we can help them face what comes next. My dad's experience makes me think about how often people try to hold everything together because they do not see another option. They keep going until the pressure becomes too much, and only then does help enter the picture. Perhaps we can learn to recognize the need sooner, both in ourselves and in the people we love. We do not have to wait until everything falls apart before admitting that we need someone beside us. Sometimes asking for help begins with acknowledging that life has changed and we do not yet know how to manage everything that change has brought. And sometimes the answer to that need comes through another person who is willing to help us find our footing again.
