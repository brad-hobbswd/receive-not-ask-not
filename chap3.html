Chapter Three

The Things We Carry Alone

Some people become so accustomed to being needed that they forget they are allowed to need something, too. They are the ones others call when something goes wrong. They find a way to keep the household moving, make sure responsibilities are handled, and offer support when someone else is struggling. From the outside, they may look dependable and strong. What is less visible is the weight they carry when no one thinks to ask how they are doing.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that can grow inside a life filled with responsibility. It is not necessarily the loneliness of having no one around. It can happen while surrounded by family, friends, coworkers, and people who genuinely appreciate what you do. You may be present for everyone else and still feel that there is very little room to say, honestly, that you are tired or that you need help.

For some, this pattern begins early. A child may learn to pay attention to what the family needs before having the words or freedom to recognize what is happening inside. Responsibilities arrive, and the child adapts. There may be schoolwork to finish, siblings to help, a parent who is unwell, or a home that still needs care. The child does what seems necessary because the needs are real and the people involved matter.

As the years pass, that way of living can become familiar. New seasons bring new responsibilities, but the role remains much the same. A person becomes a caregiver, a partner, a parent, a dependable family member, or the one who knows how to solve the problem. They learn to keep moving even when life is asking more of them than they know how to explain.

Being dependable is not a weakness. Caring for people is not a mistake, and responsibility can be a meaningful expression of love. The difficulty begins when responsibility leaves no room for honesty about our own limits. We can start believing that asking for help would disappoint people, make us look incapable, or take attention away from someone whose needs seem more urgent.

Sometimes we do not ask because we have convinced ourselves that we should already know how to handle everything. Sometimes we do not ask because no one has taught us how to name what we need. And sometimes we are so practiced at responding to other people's needs that we do not recognize our own until exhaustion, stress, or pain makes them impossible to ignore.

This is where the subject of asking becomes deeply personal. It is one thing to believe that God invites us to bring our needs before Him. It is another thing to accept that the invitation includes us, especially when we have spent much of our lives focusing on everybody else.

When Being Needed Becomes Who We Are

There is a difference between being someone others can depend on and believing that our value depends on being needed. The first can be a gift. The second can become a burden. If our identity is built entirely around what we provide, we may struggle when we have nothing left to give. We may feel guilty resting, uncomfortable receiving care, or uncertain how to respond when someone asks what we need.

We can also confuse silence with strength. We may tell ourselves that we are protecting others by keeping our struggles to ourselves. There are times when discretion is wise, and not every person is safe to trust with every part of our story. But carrying everything alone should not become the only way we know how to live. Wisdom includes recognizing trustworthy people and allowing appropriate support into our lives.

Scripture does not present human beings as people who were designed to carry every burden in isolation. Galatians 6:2 calls believers to bear one another's burdens. That instruction reminds us that support is not meant to move in only one direction. There are seasons when we help someone else, and there are seasons when we need someone to help us. Receiving care does not make our previous acts of service less meaningful.

We see this principle in the invitation Jesus gives in Matthew 11:28. He calls those who are weary and burdened to come to Him for rest. The invitation is not limited to people who have failed or those who have reached a breaking point. It is an invitation to people who are tired. We do not have to prove that we can carry one more thing before we are allowed to come to God.

Asking Does Not Mean We Have Failed

For a person who has spent years taking care of others, asking may feel unfamiliar. It can feel easier to offer help than to explain why help is needed. Yet asking is not the opposite of responsibility. It is one way of taking responsibility for our own well-being. It allows us to be truthful about what we can manage and what requires support.

That support may take different forms. We may need to pray honestly, speak with someone we trust, ask family members to share responsibilities, seek pastoral care, or reach out to a qualified counselor or therapist. Practical help and spiritual dependence are not competing choices. Seeking support does not mean that prayer has failed, and prayer does not require us to refuse the help available to us.

There is also a difference between asking God to help us and asking Him to make us capable of enduring absolutely everything without assistance. We sometimes pray for strength while refusing the very support that could help us move forward. We ask for relief but remain silent about what is happening. We ask God to change our circumstances while believing we must continue pretending that those circumstances are not affecting us.

Honest prayer gives us room to tell the truth. We can say that we are tired. We can admit that we do not know what to do next. We can ask for wisdom, provision, healing, patience, or the courage to speak with someone who can help. We do not need polished words to make a sincere request.

The Person Who Helps Others Needs Care, Too

When I considered my own experiences, I recognized how early responsibility had shaped the way I responded to life. There were family needs, education, relationships, work, and seasons when people around me needed support. I kept trying to show up. Over time, I also had to acknowledge that I had struggles of my own and that I needed support rather than simply pushing through every difficult season.

My conversation with Justin brought that realization into clearer focus. He asked whether I had ever felt that I needed to be strong for everyone else while struggling myself. As I answered, I reflected on how that pattern had followed me from childhood into adulthood. I spoke about family responsibilities, caring for people I loved, navigating relationships, and eventually recognizing that therapy and attention to my own health were important parts of my life.

That conversation was not about presenting myself as someone who had everything figured out. It was an opportunity to acknowledge that being there for others does not remove our own need for care. I could love people, take responsibility, and still admit that I needed help. I could recognize difficult chapters without allowing them to define the whole story of my life.

Justin's questions helped me see that the person everyone depends on may be the very person who has the hardest time asking. Sometimes the first step is not finding the perfect words. It is recognizing that our needs matter and that we are not required to disappear behind our responsibilities.

Learning to Bring Our Needs Before God

James 4:2 challenges us to recognize the importance of asking. But before we apply that instruction to someone else, we should consider what makes asking difficult. For the dependable person, the obstacle may not be a lack of faith or a lack of desire to pray. It may be years of learning to put everyone else first, a fear of becoming a burden, or the belief that needing help is something to hide.

God's invitation does not require us to pretend that we are self-sufficient. We can bring Him the needs we have carried quietly. We can ask for help without shame, and we can take practical steps toward receiving it. We can continue to care for others while learning that our own well-being is also worth caring for.

Perhaps the question for the person who is always strong is not simply, “How much more can I carry?” Perhaps it is, “What have I been afraid to ask for, and who can I safely ask to help me carry it?”

You do not become less loving because you need care. You do not become less dependable because you acknowledge your limits. And you do not become less faithful because you bring your own needs before God.

Sometimes the next step in learning to ask is allowing yourself to be included among the people who deserve compassion, support, and prayer.

My conversation with Justin explores these reflections through my own experiences of responsibility, family, relationships, and learning to seek support. Read “The One Everyone Depends On” for the personal conversation behind this chapter.
